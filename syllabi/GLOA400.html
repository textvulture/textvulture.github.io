2 Course Description

The primary goal of this class is to get help you complete an independent research project (see Section 2.2 and Section 2.3) that has direct relevance to globalization. You will go through the entire process of completing a social science research project. In doing so, you will wrap up and revisit your course taking experience in GMU as a Global Affairs major, which is what a capstone course is really about. This will be probably the most demanding, but also possibly most rewarding, course that you take in GMU. You are responsible from the beginning to the end of your project, which will require your attention constantly. Unlike others, the class includes significant amount of one-on-one meetings as well as self-guided writing times. By presenting your own work and giving feedback to others’, the course also offers a great opportunity to train yourself for professional communication skills.

2.1 Learning Outcomes

  1. Understand knowledge creation: Students will understand how global knowledge is generated and communicated, and how it can be used to address questions or problems in disciplines and in society.
  2. Engage multiple perspectives: Students will be able to identify and negotiate multiple perspectives on globalization, work collaboratively within and across multiple contexts, and engage ethically with their subject and with others.
  3. Investigate a meaningful question: Students will use scientific inquiry skills to articulate a question; engage in an inquiry process; and situate the concepts, practices, or results within a broader context.
    • Students will be able to ask increasingly complex questions about significant global problems, debates, or challenges.
    • Students will be able to evaluate and choose research methods that are appropriate to a project.
    • Students will be able to explain how a project has value to professional, scholarly, or global contexts.
  4. Scholarly Communication: Students will be able to present the findings of their research project in an effective and communicative way and provide constructive feedback when presented with research of others.

2.2 Mason Core Synthesis

The course fulfills the Mason Core ‘Synthesis’ requirement. Upon completing this course, students are expected to be able to (required of any Mason Core synthesis course):

  1. Communicate effectively in both oral and written forms, applying appropriate rhetorical standards (e.g., audience adaptation, language, argument, organization, evidence, etc.)
  2. Using perspectives from two or more disciplines, connect issues in a given field to wider intellectual, community or societal concerns
  3. Apply critical thinking skills to evaluate the quality, credibility and limitations of an argument or a solution using appropriate evidence or resources

3.1 The topic

There’s no grade specifically assigned to what kind of topic you choose. But picking up the right topic is tremendously important and will affect the grades of other items significantly. Topics should be related to globalization, broadly defined. Consider the following when choosing your topic. You are encouraged to discuss your topic with me in advance.

  • Public: Not your or someone else’s personal experience but an event (or the news thereof) that a sizeable number of the global public was exposed to. A ‘controversy’ that happened only at a corner of the internet might not qualify. If the topic was covered quite a bit by the global media, that’s a safe bet.
  • The premise is based on verifiable facts: A story of an individual based on rumors wouldn’t cut it. Try to avoid the cases built on mere accusations. As a general rule, empirically unverifiable subjects cannot be studied scientifically.
  • At least some components of the story cross a border: Something that 1) happened solely within a country and 2) matters only to that country would not qualify. Granted, few of such one-country events exist these days. An otherwise purely domestic topic that has important and clear implications beyond the border (e.g., election; secessionist movement; populism; mass displacement; economic crisis) will also qualify.

Your project will be much more in-depth, focused, and analytical than, say, a Wikipedia entry. Limiting the time frame and/or geographical coverage would be a good strategy. A descriptive overview that reads like a ‘social commentary’ or a punditry essay should be avoided. A generic chronicling of an event is also undesirable. Be concrete, specific, and focused. This will take time and effort. And your capstone is that much more valuable.

3.2 Idea Pitch (5%)

Early in the semester, you will share your rough ideas on the potential topic of your capstone project. It’s a five-minute pitch, unstructured. Do not bring in slides. Just verbally deliver 1) what the topic you are thinking about for your project is; 2) why you think that’s interesting; 3) how you’ll go about it.

The class will be divided into Units A and B (announced a week before). When one unit gives a pitch (individually), the other has to provide verbal feedback and suggestions, focusing on how doable the proposed idea sounds. In the end, you will have provided feedback for at least two pitches. At this stage, any feedback should be highly valuable. As far as you offer one pitch, the grade will reflect mostly your feedback. The more concrete and constructive, the better.

3.3 Literature Review (5%)

  • Complete the research paper up to the literature review.
  • For a generic instruction on how to write a research paper, see this YouTube video. Not all of this is strictly applicable to what we do (refer instead to the lecture video for details of what literature review is about), but it should give you a rough idea of how to do it.
  • You are expected to turn in a mostly-finished Literature Review of your paper as well as rudimentary Introduction. What this means is that you also have a reasonably clear ‘argument’ to offer.
  • In the literature review:
    • include at least six academic sources (narrowly defined, such as academic books or peer-reviewed journal articles). You are also encouraged to use non-academic sources as much as you want including policy reports, blog posts, news or magazine articles, but they don’t count towards the 6 academic sources requirement.
    • highlight the contribution of your paper to the literature. This doesn’t have to be (or probably should not be) something grandiose. In fact, it has very little to do with ‘doing good things for the world.’ Boring stuff like ‘few people talked about X in country Y’s context’ is just fine. The important part here is to demonstrate ‘few people.’
    • include proper citations and references.
    • make sure to discuss theoretical stuff. By ‘theoretical,’ I mean general relationship between factors closely related to your project regardless of the empirical context—we will talk about this in more detail in class.
      • If, for example, your topic is a cash transfer programs in Sudan, a theoretical piece you might need to discuss is a general discussions on the effectiveness of cash transfer programs, foreign aid, or micro-credit programs in a much broader and not Sudan-specific context. This way, you can situate your project in the literature and easily identify what the possible contribution can be.

3.4 Presentations

  • The two presentations are not only important as a core step in the process of a research project, but also as a training ground where you can improve your professional communication skills. In almost all professions, these days, you will be expected to give presentations on your work, particularly when you are promoted in the organization. So why not work on that?

  • The presentation is expected to be clear and succinct (such that we can have enough Q and A). Slides are required. There’s no additional points for fancy slides. All that matters is clarity. Slides with too many or too few words wouldn’t be effective. Submit your side in the designated place in Canvas before the beginning of your presentation (so practically, before class). DO NOT SEND ME YOUR SLIDES.

  • In evaluating the presentation, I will focus on:

    • is the topic properly selected (see the ‘public, verifiable, and cross-borderly’ requirements above)?
    • is the argument (or the main point) clearly presented and convincing?
    • is the empirical evidence compelling?
    • is the overall delivery of the presentation effective, not circling around irrelevant background information?

3.4.1 Presentation #1 (5%)

The first presentation still contains all the elements of your project completed so far, but with the understanding that some of them can be incomplete. Keep your presentation to 7 minutes or shorter.

3.4.2 Presentation #2 (15%)

The second presentation assumes that your project now has all the pieces it needs, although they might need improvements. If there’s any significant change from the first presentation, acknowledge it. Keep your presentation to 10 minutes or shorter.

3.6 Research Paper (30%)

  • Due Noon, Dec 7
  • The steps taken up to this point should naturally lead to a research paper, which is the final product of the class.
  • As always, a proper citation style (e.g., APA) is required. No footnote citations (it’s lame!). Instead, follow ‘(author-year)’ citation.
  • There’s no particular reference style required, but use a generally accepted one (e.g., Chicago, APA, Harvard) consistently.
  • The paper should not be longer than 5,000 words inclusive of the references. It should be longer than 3000 words.

    • Literature Review
    • Argument/Theory/Hypothesis
    • Empirics (Data and Analysis)

  • You have numerous templates for this. Most of the article-form readings you have had in this class–and other!–are great examples of research papers. Because you’ve regularly completed readings in these courses that you took, you are expected to be very familiar with what a research paper looks like.

In particular, participating in class discussions will help you navigate your own research topics and refine the research design you come up with. Try to approach other people’s project in the context of ‘what would I have done with that?’

In practical terms, both frequency and quality matter. There are numerous ways to offer high-quality comments. In general though, the more, the better.

4.4 Use of Artificial Intelligence

A Large Language Model (LLM), commonly referred to as AI, is a double-edged sword, in that it can help you research better but doing so could easily involve plagiarism/cheating. As a general rule, making an LLM ‘write,’ be it partial or entirely, is considered unequivocal cheating (‘consulting unauthorized materials’) and a direct violation of GMU Academic Standard, which applies to you even if you’re unaware of it. Doing so also risks committing plagiarism because your using something that does not belong to you without a citation. The writing itself has to be yours and yours only. Note that a ‘light’ rephrasing (e.g., altering some words) is still plagiarism. Similarly, submission of an edited version of LLM-generated writing is still considered plagiarism. That means if you ask an LLM to generate a rough draft and then submit something that you revised heavily it still counts as a violation of the Academic Standard.

LLMs could be used to employed in the process of research. A good example is to use an LLM in finding topics. For example, you want to work on foreign aid coming in the form of providing portable water (i.e., constructing wells), which you read an article about, but do not really know where to start. Ask LLMs to give you a list of successful and unsuccessful cases of such projects. Then you can start searching for the existing research on those specific cases.

This won’t be a simple, over-night task. There’s going to be hours spent in refining prompts and coming back to the drawing board many times. The more you dig in, the more useful the outcome. One way to see if you are using LLM properly is to see how much time and energy you’re spending on it. My own use of LLM in my research is to run an iterative code that gives prompts to an LLM (using tokens) to determine the ideological leanings of millions of news headlines. I would have needed a lot of research fund to pull this through.

If it takes a lot of effort and committing many hours, you are probably using LLM right. It gets you farther than you might have without it. If what it does is simply make your life more comfortable or save you from the deadline, I’d say there’s a very good chance that you’re violating the Honor Code). More importantly, by doing so, you pay quite a bit of money for a valuable research/training opportunity and then just decline it while throwing away your conscience (and potentially throwing yourself under the bus if caught).

Not bothering using an LLM in the first place remains a great option.

4.5 Mutual Respect & Collegiality

You go to college to expose yourself to people who disagree with you. Exchanges of intellectual ideas in a safe and free environment are integral to our learning. However, this should not come at the expense of civility, which is a value of a higher order. No speech or action intended to result in—or in fact resulting in—mental or physical attacks on others (e.g., a statement denying the existence or human dignity of a group of people; a speech promoting violence) will be tolerated in this classroom. You are free to hold such views, but that freedom does not override others’ right to feel safe in this class.

4.6 Institutional Review Board

If your research involves non-human subjects such as historical sources, public data, news articles, you can just ignore this part. If you use public information, this subsection doesn’t concern you either even if it’s about humans (e.g., social media posts; online comments), although it is generally recommended that you anonymize the human sources in these cases.

If you are to use a research or data collection method involving a human subject (e.g., human-facing experiment, interview, focus group, or participatory observation), you need to go through Institutional Review Board (IRB) review with the university. A project that is strictly contained within a classroom, IRB could be waived. However, GLOA 400 is an IMPACT+ course, which requires the findings of all research projects to be shared outside the classroom. By definition, therefore, you will need an IRB review if your research involves a human subject.

IRB takes time, sometimes stretching through months (thorugh rarely). If you’re planning on human subject research and IRB is necessary, it is strongly recommended that you file the review within the first three weeks of the semester. The system requires you to submit many different materials at the time of application, so you need to have a pretty good understanding of how you go about your project by then.

4.7.1 You ‘earn’ grades.

A word on what a grade means in this class, because it’s different from what you’re used to, say, in a stat or econ course. There, a problem set has a correct answer, and your grade is what’s left after points are deducted for departures from it. Here there is no correct answer to depart from. A grade is not what remains after subtraction; it is what your paper earns. Two papers can both be free of identifiable problems and still receive different scores, because one of them did something the other didn’t. So the question “I fixed everything you told me to fix—why isn’t this a 100?” doesn’t quite make sense. Addressing my comments addresses shortcomings, but it does not by itself produce a perfection. The top of the scale is reserved for insight. Note also what this means in the other direction: I can’t hand you a checklist that guarantees full marks, and I won’t pretend otherwise. What I can do is tell you where a draft is thin, what a sharper version of your question would look like, and which parts of your evidence aren’t carrying the weight you think they are. I can help you get better than before. To be great, however, depends solely on the steps you make.

4.8.1 Academic Standards

Mason’s academic standards rest on three principles:

  • Honesty: give accurate information in all coursework, communications, and exams.
  • Acknowledgement: credit every contribution to your work—ideas, words, and materials from others, cited in the style of the discipline—including shared authorship on group work.
  • Uniqueness of work: submit only your own original effort. This covers writing, code, presentations, and exams, and it rules out self-plagiarism.

Plagiarism, fabrication, and cheating are among the violations handled under University procedures, and sanctions can be academic, disciplinary, or both.

6 Online Data Resources

Rarely do I see, while teaching this class, a quantitatively oriented research project. That’s fine and understandable (because few people are asked to take a quantitative method class in GLOA). More often than not, though, people are just scared of using even a tiny bit of numerical data because of the inconvenience of learning statistical packages (or ‘programs’ such as R, Stata, or Matlab).

This is a shame because using some data could easily enrich the depth of your otherwise qualitative case study. Often it helps you contextualize the importance of your topic on a broader scope. Luckily, modern social science data sets tend to offer various online tools available. You don’t need to learn any statistics (though that’d be awesome) to retrieve some data relevant to your topic. Even better, you can generate cool-looking graphs simply using some drop-down menus. I suggest that you at least play with these data sets.

  • World Development Indicators (WDI)
    • any development-related indicators (not only economic and financial, but also social, public health, and gender-related variables) for almost all countries in the world over many years
  • World Values Survey (WVS)
    • individual-level survey data on various topics (social, cultural, political, and economic) covering multiple (but not very often) years for many countries
  • Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem)
    • political (particularly very detailed democracy-related ones) and social (e.g., corruption) data for almost all countries in the world for a very extensive period of time
